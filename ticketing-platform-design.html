System design proposal · 2026-09-20

Concert ticketing that survives the on-sale

Two million fans want sixty thousand seats at 10:00:00. The design below does not try to make the purchase path handle two million people. It makes the edge handle them, and lets a bounded, deliberately chosen number through to a core that is small, boring, and strictly consistent.

The three ideas
  1. The waiting room converts demand into a number we pick. Everything behind it is sized for the admission rate (~50 shoppers/s steady), not for the crowd (~100k req/s).
  2. One arbiter for a seat: a conditional UPDATE in Postgres. Caches may be stale and workers may crash; none of them can oversell, because none of them decide.
  3. A feedback loop closes the system. When holds slow down, the payment provider browns out, or inventory runs dry, the admission rate drops — to zero if needed — and fans wait in a queue instead of hammering a failing checkout.

The data is tiny; the problem is contention and burst, not volume.

3 · Life of a seat

    

      The no-oversell guarantee lives in this state machine, enforced by Postgres. The one subtle state is
      payment unknown: when the provider times out we do not know whether the card was charged,
      so the seats stay held until a reconciler finds out. Releasing them on timeout is how platforms end up
      with charged customers and no tickets.
    

    

      
    

    
What makes it hold

    
    
      
  • Hold is one conditional statement over every requested seat. If the row count is short, the transaction rolls back and the shopper gets 409 seat_taken. Expiry is lazy — it is part of the predicate, so no timer infrastructure can fail.
    • 
      
  • Confirm flips order and seats in one transaction, WHERE hold_id = $1. If the hold was lost, the authorisation is voided and never captured. A unique constraint on order_items.seat_id is the backstop.
    • 
      
  • Every mutating call carries an idempotency key, and the payment provider's key is the order id, so client retries and queue redeliveries cannot double-hold or double-charge.
    •

      Recommendation: A. The waiting room makes the write load a number we set, and that number
      (~400 hold attempts/s, 10× headroom = 4k) sits inside what one Postgres primary does with short
      single-statement transactions. General-admission tickets are pre-materialised as rows and allocated with
      FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, so there is no hot counter row.
      What would change my mind: a load test showing hold p99 over 300 ms at 4k attempts/s with
      30% of attempts aimed at the same 500 seats, or a product requirement to sell without a queue.

5 · One-way doors

    

      
⚠ CRITICAL PATH · DB schema
Seat-per-row inventory, partitioned by event
One row per sellable unit, event_id as partition and shard key. Everything contended hangs off this; changing it under live inventory means a dual-write migration.

      
⚠ CRITICAL PATH · public promise
Fairness policy
Login required, one position per account, random draw for everyone present at T0, FIFO after, per-account seat limits. Once announced, changing it is a trust and possibly legal matter, not a deploy.

      
⚠ CRITICAL PATH · payments contract
Authorise-then-capture, client-side tokenisation
Sets PCI scope (card data never reaches us) and makes "void on lost hold" possible. Switching to direct capture later would remove the safety net under the confirm step.

      
⚠ SECURITY · second review
Admission token and webhooks
Token signing keys, claims (account, event, expiry, kid, version), session binding, and provider webhook signature checks. Versioned, so reversible — but a forged token skips the queue, so it needs a second reviewer.

    

    
Two-way doors: the queue vendor, Redis as the availability view (derived data, rebuildable), and the queue technology.

7 · Rollout, rollback, verification

    
    
      
  1. Prove the arbiter first. Inventory module alone, 60k seats, 4k hold attempts/s with 30% aimed at the same 500 seats. Pass: zero oversold seats, p99 < 300 ms.
    2. 
      
  2. Synthetic on-sale at 2× the numbers above, bot traffic included, against a production-shaped shard. Pass: origin request rate tracks admitted shoppers, not demand.
    3. 
      
  3. Game days: kill Redis; 10 s provider brownout; Postgres failover mid-sale; kill the relay. Pass: invariants hold, the controller drops the release rate to zero and recovers with no operator action.
    4. 
      
  4. Ramp by event size behind a per-event flag: club shows, then arenas, then a stadium.
    5. 
      
  5. Rollback is a per-event kill switch: release rate zero plus a "paused" page, with holds auto-extended while paused. The system is greenfield, so there is no data to migrate back; later schema changes go expand/contract.
    6. 
    

    

      !
      
None of the throughput figures here are measured. They are sizing assumptions with stated arithmetic; step 1 is what turns the central one — that a single Postgres primary comfortably arbitrates an admission-capped on-sale — into a fact or a reason to revisit option C.
